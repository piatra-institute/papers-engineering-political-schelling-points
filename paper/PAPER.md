## Abstract

When many accounts amplify a call to collective action, the message matters as evidence that the action can succeed and as a shared reference that lets people predict one another. A regime-change global game separates the two: a message's perceived precision depends on how independent its sources are believed to be, and a reach parameter measures whether its receipt is common knowledge. The equilibrium cutoff decomposes exactly into a closed-form evidential channel and a coordination channel equal to an explicit, reach-dependent multiplier on it; at baseline coordination is 80% of the effect when receipt is common knowledge. A message every receiver believes uninformative has no effect however visible, but a receiver who alone learns that the source is common keeps following it because others do. Disclosing a common source in isolation removes 11% of the effect of a campaign of 3 accounts at full reach; as common knowledge it removes 71%; when it also makes receipt common knowledge it raises participation in a mobilizing message, to first order, exactly when a publicity-credibility ratio exceeds one. If participation erodes the status quo, a temporary message persists only above a closed-form feedback threshold and only if it pushes participation past an unstable state. Exposure data cannot separate persuasion, coordination and the majority illusion; a laboratory design crossing visibility with incentives can, with 7 to 74 groups of 20 per cell depending on how fully participants act on coordination. All results are exact computations in a stylised model with illustrative parameters.

## A call seen by many and sent by one

A call to stop work at noon on Friday circulates in a city's feeds, posted by eighty accounts that look unrelated. A reader can take two different things from it. The first is evidence: if eighty people independently judged that the stoppage can succeed, their judgments say something about the conditions on which success depends. The second is a reference point: a reader who believes that many others have seen the same call can expect them to act on it, which makes noon on Friday the obvious moment to act. Schelling called references of the second kind focal points [@schelling1960]. A day later the platform reports that the eighty accounts were run from one source. The report removes most of the evidence, because eighty copies of one judgment carry the information of one. Its effect on the reference point depends on who learns of it. A reader who sees a label on the post and assumes that others did not can still expect them to act on the call. If the report is itself public news, every reader learns that the source was single and also that the call reached everyone.

This paper asks how much of a message's effect on participation runs through each of these two channels, how disclosing a common source changes each, and when a temporary message leaves a change that outlasts it. It treats the mechanism analytically, as an object of measurement and of institutional defence; it says nothing about how such campaigns are produced.

## What is already known, and what is not

### Public information in global games

Carlsson and van Damme showed that small private noise about payoffs selects a unique equilibrium in coordination games [@carlsson1993], and Morris and Shin showed that a continuum of speculators with private signals has a unique equilibrium in a model of self-fulfilling currency attacks [@morris1998]. In the static regime-change game with a normal prior of precision $\alpha$ and normal private signals of precision $\beta$, the equilibrium is unique if and only if $\beta \ge \alpha^2/(2\pi)$, and the success threshold converges to $1-c$ as private noise vanishes [@angeletos2007, p. 719]. Hellwig showed that multiple equilibria become possible when the overall level of noise is low and public information is relatively informative [@hellwig2002]. In a beauty-contest economy Morris and Shin showed that agents give the public signal more weight than its relative precision, a reflection of the coordination motive, and that when that motive is strong more precise public information lowers welfare if private information is precise enough [@morris2002]. Their survey calls the disproportionate impact of public information a publicity effect and notes that its multiplier is largest just before the parameters enter the region of multiple equilibria [@morris2003]. Angeletos, Hellwig and Pavan showed that the information generated by a policy maker's intervention restores multiple equilibria [@angeletos2006], and that in a dynamic regime-change game with learning, multiplicity can arise under the same conditions that guarantee uniqueness in the static game, with phases of tranquillity and of attack that occur without changes in fundamentals [@angeletos2007].

### Regime change, protest and manipulation

Edmond studied a regime that can engage in propaganda that, taken at face value, makes it seem stronger than it is; holding the number of signals fixed, the regime is harder to overthrow when each signal is precise, while adding signals, which raises both their total precision and the regime's cost of manipulation, makes it easier to overthrow unless information control has strong economies of scale [@edmond2013]. Little showed that making grievances more public has an ambiguous effect on protest, because citizens can learn that the regime is more or less popular than they expected, whereas information about logistics almost always encourages protest [@little2016]. In a second model, citizens who are not credulous still respond to propaganda because of the coordination motive, and when that motive dominates they mimic the credulous [@little2017]. Chen, Lu and Suen showed that a rumour creates a public topic on which citizens exchange views, that discussion can make a rumour more mobilizing than if everyone fully believed it, and that an extreme rumour can backfire [@chen2016]. Barberà and Jackson argued that costly protests reveal willingness to act more reliably than cheaper communication such as social media [@barbera2020]. Bueno de Mesquita modelled a vanguard whose violence manipulates beliefs about the level of anti-government sentiment; his mechanism is informational, and his solution concept rules out the vanguard creating focal points [@buenodemesquita2010].

### Information design

Bayesian persuasion and its many-player generalisation characterise what a sender who commits to an information structure can induce [@kamenica2011; @bergemann2019]. For binary-action supermodular games, Morris, Oyama and Takahashi show that implementing an outcome as the smallest equilibrium requires a sequential obedience condition, and that some outcomes require private signals [@morris2024]. Inostroza and Pavan show that, among public disclosures, the policy of a designer who fears the worst equilibrium removes strategic uncertainty by inducing all agents to take the same action [@inostroza2025]. Cornand and Heinemann studied a public signal given to each agent with some probability and the optimal degree of that publicity in a beauty-contest economy [@cornand2008]. The present paper does not solve a designer's problem. It takes a message as given and asks what its provenance and its visibility do to its effect, which is the question an observer, a platform or a regulator faces when measuring a campaign and deciding how to disclose its source.

### Common knowledge, thresholds and preference falsification

Chwe showed that a communication network helps coordination by informing later stages about earlier ones and by creating common knowledge within each stage [@chwe2000], and argued that public rituals and media events serve to produce common knowledge [@chwe2001]. Kuran argued that by suppressing their opposition to the status quo, East Europeans misled everyone, themselves included, about the chances of a successful uprising, which is why the 1989 revolutions came as a surprise [@kuran1991; @kuran1995]. Granovetter's threshold model showed that small changes in the distribution of individual thresholds can switch a population between little and wide participation [@granovetter1978], and Brock and Durlauf showed that a mean-field model of discrete choice with social interactions has multiple equilibria when the interactions exceed a threshold [@brock2001].

### Correlation neglect and the majority illusion

DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel defined persuasion bias as a failure to account for possible repetition in the information one receives [@demarzo2003]. Enke and Zimmermann found that, on average, experimental subjects treat correlated information partially as independent and so double-count a common source [@enke2019]. Eyster and Rabin modelled players who underestimate how other players' actions depend on their information [@eyster2005] and people who believe that each predecessor's action reflects only that person's private information [@eyster2010]. Lerman, Yan and Wu showed that network structure alone can make an attribute held by a minority appear to be held by a majority in many people's neighbourhoods, especially when low-degree nodes connect to high-degree ones [@lerman2016].

### Evidence on exposure, norms and focal points

Eady and colleagues linked 1496 survey respondents to the posts in their Twitter timelines around the 2016 United States election; exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency's accounts was concentrated, with 1% of users accounting for 70% of exposures, and they found no evidence of a meaningful relationship between exposure and changes in attitudes, polarization or voting behaviour [@eady2023]. Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott found that most young married men in Saudi Arabia privately support women working outside the home and underestimate other men's support, and that correcting these beliefs increased their costly willingness to help their wives search for jobs [@bursztyn2020]. Crawford, Gneezy and Rottenstreich found that with even slightly asymmetric payoffs, salient labels lose much of their power to coordinate [@crawford2008]. In the laboratory, Heinemann, Nagel and Ockenfels found that subjects use threshold strategies in a global game and that coordination points are about as predictable with public as with private information [@heinemann2004], and Cornand and Heinemann found that subjects put more weight on public than on private signals, but less than equilibrium predicts [@cornand2014].

### What this paper adds

The overweighting of public signals, the manipulation of signals by a regime, the leverage that credulous citizens give propaganda over sophisticated ones, the mobilizing power of a rumour that becomes a public topic and the neglect of correlation are all established. What is missing is a model in which a message's provenance and its visibility are separate variables, so that the disclosure of a common source, which acts on the first, can be analysed together with the publicity of the disclosure, which acts on the second. The paper contributes five things: an exact decomposition of a message's effect on the equilibrium cutoff into a closed-form evidential channel and a coordination channel with an explicit, reach-dependent multiplier; a theorem giving the sign of a disclosure's effect when it is seen in isolation, when it is common knowledge, and when it also makes receipt common knowledge; a closed-form feedback threshold for persistence in a repeated version; an identification result; and a laboratory design with model-derived effect sizes and sample sizes.

## The model

### Players, payoffs and information

A continuum of citizens of mass one chooses whether to participate in a collective action. The action succeeds if and only if the participating share $A$ is at least $\theta$, the strength of the status quo. A participant receives $1-c$ if the action succeeds and $-c$ if it fails, with $0<c<1$; abstaining pays nothing. The prior is $\theta \sim N(\mu, 1/\alpha)$, and citizen $i$ observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1/\beta)$, independent across citizens.

### Provenance as perceived precision

A message $z$ reports on $\theta$. It is the average of $n$ reports $s_k = \theta + u_k$, each with error variance $1/\rho$ and pairwise error correlation $r$, so its precision is

$$\tau = \frac{n\rho}{1+(n-1)r}.$$

Independent sources ($r=0$) give $n\rho$; one source repeated by $n$ accounts ($r=1$) gives $\rho$. A receiver who believes the correlation is $\hat r$ uses the perceived precision $\hat\tau$ obtained by replacing $r$ with $\hat r$. Correlation neglect of degree $\chi \in [0,1]$ means $\hat r = (1-\chi) r$. Provenance enters the model only through perceived precision.

### Reach and shared visibility

The message reaches a share $v$ of citizens. Receivers may differ in perceived precision: group $j$ has mass $w_j$ and perceived precision $\tau_j$, with $\sum_j w_j = v$. The masses and perceived precisions of all groups are common knowledge among receivers, so $v=1$ means that receipt is common knowledge in the whole population and $v$ near zero means that each receiver believes herself one of very few. Non-receivers do not condition on the message and play the equilibrium of the game without it. The experimental section checks this simplification against a common-prior version in which non-receivers know that a message was shown to a share $v$ but not what it said. Reach is the paper's measure of shared visibility; it is distinct from the number of accounts, which acts only through $\hat\tau$.

### Equilibrium

Write $\Phi$ and $\phi$ for the standard normal distribution and density. Without a message, the success threshold $\theta_0$ and the participation cutoff $k_0$ satisfy

$$\Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt\beta}(\theta_0-\mu) - \sqrt{1+\alpha/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c), \qquad k_0 = \theta_0 + \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta,$$

and citizens participate when $x_i \le k_0$ [@angeletos2007]. With a message, a group-$j$ receiver participates when $x_i \le k_j$. Define

$$g_j(\theta) = \frac{\alpha(\theta-\mu)+\tau_j(\theta-z)}{\sqrt\beta} - \sqrt{\frac{\alpha+\tau_j+\beta}{\beta}}\,\Phi^{-1}(c),$$

$$S(\theta) = (1-v)\,\Phi\big(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta)\big) + \sum_j w_j\,\Phi\big(g_j(\theta)\big) - \theta.$$

**Lemma 1.** In every equilibrium in cutoff strategies the action succeeds if and only if $\theta \le \theta^*$, where $S(\theta^*)=0$, and each group's cutoff is $k_j = \theta^* + g_j(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$. A root exists in $[0,1]$, and it is unique when $\sum_j w_j(\alpha+\tau_j) < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$; with one group of full reach this is the classic condition $(\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta \le \sqrt{2\pi}$.

The proof (appendix) uses the fact that each group's indifference condition pins its cutoff to $\theta^*$, so the equilibrium reduces to one equation however many receiver groups there are. The receivers' cutoff is the measure of participation used throughout: the share of receivers who participate at any fundamental rises with it. Effects are reported in percentage points of receivers' participation at $\theta=\theta_0$, where participation without the message equals $\theta_0$.

The baseline sets $\alpha = 4$, $\mu = 0.6$, $\beta = 25$ and $c = 0.5$: the public expects the action to need 60% participation, with a prior standard deviation of 0.5, and each citizen's private signal is more precise than the prior. One report has precision $\rho = 1$. Without a message, $\theta_0 = 0.453$, so the action succeeds with prior probability 0.385. The baseline mobilizing message reports $z = \theta_0 - 0.3$.

## Evidential and coordination channels

The message's effect on a receiver's cutoff has two parts. The evidential channel is the change in the cutoff of a receiver who updates her belief about $\theta$ but expects everyone else to behave as without the message; it equals the effect at reach zero. The coordination channel is the rest: the additional change that occurs because others who saw the message also respond to it, and she knows it.

**Proposition 1.** Take one receiver group with perceived precision $\tau>0$ and reach $v \in (0,1]$, with a unique equilibrium. Let

$$z_0(\tau) = \theta_0 - \Phi^{-1}(c)\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\beta}}{\tau}.$$

(i) For every reach, $k - k_0$ has the sign of $z_0(\tau) - z$ and is zero at $z = z_0(\tau)$: a message reporting a weaker status quo than $z_0$ raises participation, a message reporting a stronger one lowers it.

(ii) The evidential channel is $E = \tau\,[z_0(\tau)-z]/\beta$.

(iii) The coordination channel $C = k - k_0 - E$ has the sign of $E$, and $|C|$ increases strictly with reach.

(iv) The cutoff's response to content is $\partial k/\partial z = -(\tau/\beta)\,M$, where

$$M = 1 + \frac{v\,\phi_R\,(\alpha+\tau+\beta)}{\sqrt\beta + (1-v)\,\beta\,\phi_N - v\,\phi_R\,(\alpha+\tau)},$$

with $\phi_R = \phi(g(\theta^*))$ and $\phi_N = \phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*))$. At $z=z_0$ both densities equal $\phi_0 = \phi(\Phi^{-1}(\theta_0))$, which gives a closed form $M_0(\tau,v)$.

The term $\tau/\beta$ is the Bayesian weight on the message relative to the private signal; $M$ multiplies it. It equals one at zero reach and rises with reach, with private precision, and as $\alpha+\tau$ approaches the uniqueness bound, where the denominator vanishes. At full reach and the neutral message it reduces to $M_0 = (1+\sqrt\beta\,\phi_0)/(1-\phi_0(\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta)$. It is the regime-change counterpart of the overweighting of public signals in Morris and Shin's beauty contest [@morris2002] and of the publicity multiplier in their survey [@morris2003], written as a function of reach.

At baseline, with $\tau = 1$, the evidential channel raises receivers' participation by 2.38 percentage points. At reach 0.25 the total effect is 2.98 points and the coordination channel is 20% of it; at reach 0.5, 3.98 points and 40%; at full reach, 11.83 points and 80% (Figure 1a). The exact multiplier at the baseline message is 4.98 at full reach, against 4.94 for the closed form at the neutral message. Across 2592 parameter combinations (two prior precisions, three private precisions, three costs, two prior means, three message precisions, six message contents and four reaches) the sign result, the closed form for $E$, the sign and monotonicity of $C$ and the multiplier formula held without exception; the largest error of the closed-form evidential channel was $3.9 \times 10^{-16}$ and the largest relative error of the multiplier against finite differences was $3.4 \times 10^{-8}$. In addition, 20 equilibria were checked by computing best responses from a numerical integral of the posterior and iterating them from "nobody participates" and "everybody participates"; in this supermodular game the two sequences bound every equilibrium, and both converged to the analytic cutoffs within $5.3 \times 10^{-10}$.

**Proposition 2.** (i) If every receiver assigns the message zero precision, every cutoff equals $k_0$ for every reach. (ii) As $\tau \to 0$, both channels vanish but $C/E \to M_0(0,v)-1 > 0$. (iii) A single receiver who assigns precision $\tau_l$ while the others assign $\tau_h$ has cutoff $k_l = k_h - [(\tau_h-\tau_l)(\theta^*-z) - \Phi^{-1}(c)(\sqrt{\alpha+\tau_h+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\tau_l+\beta})]/\beta$ and response to content $\partial k_l/\partial z = (1+(\alpha+\tau_l)/\beta)\,\partial\theta^*/\partial z - \tau_l/\beta$, which is negative even at $\tau_l = 0$ whenever $v>0$ and $\tau_h>0$.

Part (i) is the global-game reason why a known sunspot cannot coordinate when the equilibrium is unique: a message believed uninformative is ignored by everyone, so it predicts nothing. Coordination value therefore multiplies evidential value; it cannot exist without it somewhere in the belief hierarchy. Part (iii) locates where it can survive: a receiver who regards the message as worthless still follows it because the others, who have not learned what she has, follow it. This is the mechanism of Little's credulous citizens [@little2017], here produced by provenance information that is not common knowledge. At baseline, with $\tau = 0.0001$, the computed ratio $C/E$ is 3.364 against the limit 3.364. The closed form in (iii) matched the computed cutoffs to within $4.2 \times 10^{-17}$.

## Disclosure of a common source

Let disclosure lower receivers' perceived precision from $\tau_h$ to $\tau_l$. For a campaign of $n$ accounts repeating one report under full correlation neglect, $\tau_h = n\rho$ and $\tau_l = \rho$.

**Theorem 1 (publicity paradox).**

(a) *Isolated disclosure.* A receiver who alone learns the source changes her cutoff by the closed-form amount in Proposition 2(iii), at an unchanged success threshold. For $c = 1/2$ the change never reverses the sign of the message's effect.

(b) *Common disclosure at unchanged reach.* If all receivers learn the source and this is common knowledge among them, then for $c = 1/2$ participation falls if $z < \theta_0$, is unchanged if $z = \theta_0$ and rises if $z > \theta_0$. For any $c$, the cutoff is single-peaked in perceived precision when $c<1/2$ and single-troughed when $c>1/2$, so disclosure lowers (raises) participation whenever $\partial k/\partial\tau$ is positive (negative) at both $\tau_l$ and $\tau_h$.

(c) *Public disclosure that raises reach.* If disclosure also raises reach from $v_0$ to $v_1$, define the publicity-credibility ratio

$$\Pi = \frac{\tau_l\, M_0(\tau_l, v_1)}{\tau_h\, M_0(\tau_h, v_0)}.$$

For $c = 1/2$, $\partial[k(\tau_l,v_1) - k(\tau_h,v_0)]/\partial z = (\tau_h M_0(\tau_h,v_0) - \tau_l M_0(\tau_l,v_1))/\beta$ at $z = \theta_0$. Hence for messages near $\theta_0$ disclosure amplifies the message's effect, raising participation in a mobilizing message and lowering it in a demobilizing one, if $\Pi > 1$, attenuates it if $\Pi < 1$, and leaves it unchanged to first order if $\Pi = 1$.

(d) *Uninformative source.* If disclosure makes every receiver regard the message as uninformative and this is common knowledge, the message has no effect at any reach.

Disclosure therefore removes the evidential channel and, unless it is common knowledge, leaves most of the coordination channel in place; when it is common knowledge it removes coordination value in proportion, and when it is also public it adds coordination value by raising reach. At baseline with 3 accounts ($\tau_h = 3$) and full reach, the message raises receivers' participation by 40.66 points. An isolated disclosure that leaves the disclosed receiver with precision 1 removes 11% of this effect on her (to 36.17 points), and 17% if she regards the message as uninformative (to 33.62 points). The same disclosure made common knowledge removes 71% (to 11.83 points). At reach 0.25 an isolated disclosure removes 54% of a smaller effect, because less of it was coordination. The single-peakedness in (b) held on all 358 computed paths of 80 perceived precisions each across the parameter grid of the previous section.

Public disclosure can raise participation. With 3 accounts, a campaign seen with common knowledge among a share $v_0 = 0.25$ of citizens raises receivers' participation by 9.11 points; a public disclosure that lowers perceived precision to 1 and makes receipt common knowledge raises it to 11.83 points ($\Pi = 1.30$), while the same disclosure at unchanged reach lowers it to 2.98. With 5 accounts the campaign raises participation by 15.34 points and public disclosure lowers it to 11.83 ($\Pi = 0.76$). Whether the paradox occurs depends on how much neglect inflated credibility before disclosure: with 5 accounts it occurs whenever neglect $\chi$ is below 0.926. Figure 1c shows the region at baseline. The first-order criterion predicted the sign of the exact effect at all 1438 parameter points with surprise $|z-\theta_0| = 0.01$, and at 99.7% of 1440 points with surprise 0.3 (two prior precisions, three private precisions, two values of $\tau_l$, six deflation factors, ten values of $v_0$ and both directions of surprise). The 3 points with multiple equilibria were excluded.

## Persistence after the message is gone

A message that changes participation for as long as it lasts need not leave anything behind. A persistent change requires a feedback from today's participation to tomorrow's conditions. In the repeated version each period is the static game with public prior mean $\mu_t$, and participation erodes the status quo's expected strength:

$$\mu_{t+1} = \bar\theta - \gamma A_t.$$

The deterministic skeleton sets each period's fundamental equal to its public expectation. Without a message the period equilibrium can be indexed by $u = \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$, with $\mu(u) = \Phi(u) - (\sqrt\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, $s = \sqrt{1+\alpha/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, and participation $\psi(u) = \Phi((1+\beta/\alpha)u + \beta s/\alpha)$. Steady states are the roots of $L(u) = \mu(u) + \gamma\psi(u) - \bar\theta$, and a steady state is stable if and only if $L'(u)<0$.

**Proposition 3.** Assume static uniqueness, $\alpha < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$. (i) For $c = 1/2$, the unforced dynamics have two stable steady states if and only if

$$\gamma > \gamma_c = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}-\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$$

and $\bar\theta$ lies strictly between the values of $\mu+\gamma\psi$ at the two zeros of $L'$; otherwise the steady state is unique and globally attracting, and every temporary message's effect decays. (ii) In the bistable case a campaign that ends after period $T$ leaves the high-participation state if and only if $\mu_{T+1}$ is below the unstable steady state; for a campaign of fixed content, perceived precision and reach there is a critical content $z_c$ such that it persists if and only if $z < z_c$.

The threshold falls as public information becomes more precise relative to private information and vanishes as $\beta$ grows: in the limit of precise private signals participation is all or nothing, and any erosion creates two states. The condition plays the role of Brock and Durlauf's bistability condition for mean-field social interactions [@brock2001], in a game whose static equilibrium is unique in every period.

At baseline $\gamma_c = 0.294$, equal to the numerically computed threshold to machine precision; for $c = 0.3$ and $c = 0.7$ the numerical threshold is 0.310. On a grid of 1640 pairs $(\gamma, \bar\theta)$ the number of steady states matched the characterisation everywhere. With $\gamma = 1$ and $\bar\theta = 0.897$ the low state has participation 0.0004, the unstable state 0.353 and the high state close to one. A campaign of 4 periods with perceived precision 3 persists if its content is below $z_c = -0.070$; the threshold is sharp, in that content just below it leaves the system in the high state and content just above it returns the system to the low state (Figure 2b). Longer campaigns need less extreme content, and a campaign assigned precision 1 needs content near $-1.33$ (Figure 2c). The same campaign at $\gamma = 0.8\gamma_c$ decays to the unique steady state. Disclosure matters only before the crossing: in a campaign of 6 periods whose undisclosed version crosses the unstable state after 4 periods, the change persists if the source is disclosed after 4 or more periods and dies out if it is disclosed after 3 or fewer.

The skeleton overstates persistence. When each period's fundamental is drawn around its public expectation with the prior's own standard deviation of 0.5, the high state at baseline lasts 34 periods on average before a shock tips it back; with shocks of standard deviation 0.3 it lasts 3107 periods, and with 0.2 it never left in 50,000 periods (Figure 2d). Stronger erosion makes the states more robust: at $\gamma = 1.5$ and the prior's standard deviation the mean stay is 741 periods.

## What data can and cannot identify

**Proposition 4.** (i) In data on exposure to one campaign and subsequent action, with exposure possibly randomized, four explanations are observationally equivalent: private evidence (a message seen with no shared visibility), coordination (a message whose receipt is common knowledge), private valuation persuasion and the majority illusion (a biased sample of peers treated as representative). Each acts on exposed citizens only through their cutoff, and each can produce any cutoff in an interval around $k_0$. (ii) With many messages of known content, data from the coordination game identify the response $\tau M(\tau,v)/\beta$, but not $\tau$ and $v$ separately: the pairs giving the same response form a curve. (iii) A design that adds a task whose payoffs do not depend on others identifies $\tau/\beta$ from the response to content in that task; the ratio of the responses in the coordination and individual tasks identifies $M$; persuasion and the illusion shift participation without changing the response to content; and the illusion's shift disappears when the peer sample is representative.

At baseline the full-reach campaign with $\tau = 1$ raises exposed participation by 11.83 points. The same exposed behaviour is produced by a privately seen message with perceived precision 4.96, by persuasion that lowers the cost of participating by 0.109, or by a neglected bias of 0.060 in private signals. Along the curve of equal responses to content, precision 1 with full reach matches precision 2.85 at reach 0.5 and 4.94 at reach zero (Figure 3c). A credible message seen privately and a discredited message seen by everyone, the two states that the publicity paradox compares, cannot be told apart from responses alone.

The table shows why observational studies of exposure cannot settle the mechanism. Eady and colleagues' null finding on attitudes and votes [@eady2023] concerns a population in which exposure was concentrated in a small share of users; the model implies that an exposure effect measured at one reach does not bound the effect of the same messages when receipt is common knowledge, because the multiplier depends on reach.

## A decisive experiment

### Design

Groups of 20 participants play 30 rounds of the game with the baseline parameters as its actual distributions: in each round the computer draws $\theta$, every participant's private signal and one bulletin of precision 1. Two factors vary between groups. Incentive: in the coordination task participation pays if the group's participation share reaches $\theta$; in the individual task it pays if $\theta$ is below an announced hurdle equal to $\theta_0$, so that others' choices are irrelevant and the model's cutoff equals the evidential cutoff. Visibility: in the public condition every member sees the bulletin on a shared screen with each member's acknowledgment visible; in the partial condition a random share $q = 0.25$ of members see it and are told that this share saw it, while the others are told that a bulletin was shown to that share. Both conditions are truthful. The outcome is each receiver's choice; the estimand is the visibility-by-incentive interaction in the slope of participation on bulletin content, estimated group by group.

The model's predictions are exact equilibria: the public coordination cell from Lemma 1, the partial coordination cell from the common-prior version with informed non-receivers, the individual task in closed form. Participants are allowed to fall short of equilibrium: their cutoff is the evidential cutoff plus a fraction $\omega$ of the coordination component, with logistic decision noise (scale 0.1), participant heterogeneity (standard deviation 0.1) and group heterogeneity (0.05) in cutoffs. Laboratory studies of public and private signals find subjects reacting less to public information than equilibrium predicts [@cornand2014], which is why $\omega<1$ is the relevant range.

### Predictions and power

Under equilibrium play the response of participation to one standard deviation of bulletin content (1.12) is 12.07 points in the public coordination cell against 2.85 to 3.70 points in the other three cells (Figure 3a), and the interaction is 8.48 points. It is 4.59 points at $\omega = 0.5$ and 2.41 points at $\omega = 0.25$. Power for a two-sided 5% test with group-level slopes as observations follows from the simulated between-group variance in each cell.

### Provenance arm

A third factor tests the publicity paradox directly. The bulletin is presented as the average of $n = 5$ accounts that in fact repeat one report. In the undisclosed condition participants see only that the accounts posted; in the disclosed condition they are told that all of them copied one report. Comparing the undisclosed bulletin under partial visibility with the disclosed bulletin under public visibility reproduces the two states of Theorem 1(c). The prediction depends on correlation neglect, which the individual task measures through the response to content. Without neglect the disclosed public bulletin draws a response 8.48 points per standard deviation larger and 5 groups per condition suffice; at $\chi = 0.5$ the gain is 6.09 points with 8 groups; under full neglect the sign reverses ($\Pi = 0.76$) and the undisclosed partial bulletin draws a response 2.63 points larger, which needs 43 groups per condition. The model thus predicts a sign reversal at a neglect level that the same experiment measures.

### Approximations

Two simplifications were checked. In the common-prior version with informed non-receivers, the non-receivers' participation at $\theta_0$ moves by at most 1.06 points and receivers' predicted participation differs from the simpler model by at most 0.44 points, 1.8% of the largest effect, over two precisions, four reaches and thirteen message contents; a Monte Carlo check with 400,000 draws put the non-receivers' success probability at their cutoff at 0.5001 against the required 0.5. In groups of 20 the symmetric equilibrium of the public task, computed with the binomial distribution of others' choices, has a response to content equal to 96% of the continuum value and a participation level 10.3 points higher at $\theta_0$, because each participant's own choice counts toward the threshold; with groups of 50 the response is 98.5% of the continuum value and the level 4.2 points higher. The estimand is the response to content, which the finite group changes little.

## Implications for measurement, detection and disclosure

Measure provenance and reach separately. In the model the number of accounts acts only through perceived precision, while the coordination channel depends on reach in the sense of common knowledge of receipt. Counts of posts, accounts or impressions measure neither. An audit of a suspected campaign needs two estimates: the dependence among its sources, which methods that build coordination networks from behavioural traces shared among accounts are designed to supply [@pacheco2021], and the extent to which recipients could know that others received the same content, for instance because it appeared in a shared trending list or was rebroadcast. The second quantity is what determines the multiplier, and it is rarely measured.

Treat labels and announcements as different interventions. A label that each user sees on an item, without knowing whether others saw it, is an isolated disclosure in the sense of Theorem 1(a). It removes the evidential channel for the user who sees it and leaves the coordination channel, because the user still expects others to act on the message; at baseline it removes 11% of the effect at full reach. A disclosure that is common knowledge among those who received the message removes coordination value in proportion to evidential value, 71% at baseline. A disclosure that is public news can also raise reach, and then its effect is signed by the publicity-credibility ratio. In the model a disclosure raises reach only to the extent that it conveys the message's content to people who had not seen it or makes its receipt common knowledge; a disclosure that names the source to those who already received the message, and makes that common knowledge among them, removes coordination value without adding reach.

Detect before the crossing. In the repeated model a disclosure that arrives after participation has passed the unstable state does not undo the change, because the new state sustains itself once the message has stopped. The value of early detection is concentrated in the periods before that crossing.

Read exposure studies as reach-specific. An exposure effect estimated in a population where the message was seen privately measures the evidential channel plus a small coordination channel. It does not bound the effect of the same messages when receipt is common knowledge, and the identification result shows that observational data cannot recover the multiplier. Designs that vary visibility, or natural variation in whether content became common knowledge, are needed.

Correct impressions of independent support where receipt is common knowledge. Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott's evidence that correcting misperceived norms changes behaviour [@bursztyn2020] has a counterpart in the model: a correction that each person receives privately leaves the others' expected behaviour unchanged, so it removes less than a correction whose receipt is common knowledge. Institutions that publish verified counts of independent participation in a channel everyone knows everyone watches supply the common-knowledge form of correction.

## Scope

The model has one binary action, normal information, a continuum of citizens and cutoff strategies. Uniqueness is established among cutoff strategies under the condition of Lemma 1 and, for 20 configurations, among all strategies by iterated best responses from the extreme profiles. Non-receivers ignore the message in the main model; the common-prior version with informed non-receivers changes receivers' predicted participation by at most 1.8% of the largest effect. Reach is common knowledge among receivers; intermediate higher-order structures, such as receivers who are unsure whether others know that they saw the message, are not modelled. The message is exogenous: the sender's choice of content, timing and amplification is not modelled, competing messages are not modelled, and the paper makes no welfare claims. The exact global statements about disclosure are for $c = 1/2$; for other costs the paper gives the single-peakedness lemma and computes the rest. The repeated model's persistence holds exactly in the deterministic skeleton and only approximately when the fundamental is subject to shocks of the size the agents expect. The parameter values are illustrative and are not calibrated to any population; the shares and magnitudes reported describe the baseline only. The experiment is a design: its behavioural parameters are assumptions, and its predictions are model derivations.

## Appendix: proofs

**Lemma 1.** Fix cutoffs $k_j$ for receivers and $k_0$ for non-receivers. Participation $A(\theta) = (1-v)\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta)) + \sum_j w_j\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_j-\theta))$ is strictly decreasing in $\theta$, so $A(\theta)-\theta$ has a unique zero $\hat\theta$ and the action succeeds if and only if $\theta \le \hat\theta$. A group-$j$ receiver with signal $x$ has posterior $\theta \mid x,z \sim N((\alpha\mu+\tau_j z+\beta x)/P_j,\,1/P_j)$ with $P_j = \alpha+\tau_j+\beta$, so her probability of success, $\Phi(\sqrt{P_j}(\hat\theta - (\alpha\mu+\tau_j z+\beta x)/P_j))$, is strictly decreasing in $x$, and her best response is the cutoff at which it equals $c$: $\beta k_j = P_j\hat\theta - \alpha\mu - \tau_j z - \sqrt{P_j}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, that is, $\sqrt\beta(k_j-\hat\theta) = g_j(\hat\theta)$. Substituting into $A(\hat\theta) = \hat\theta$ gives $S(\hat\theta) = 0$; conversely, for any root $\theta^*$ of $S$ the cutoffs $\theta^* + g_j(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ are mutual best responses. $S$ is continuous with $S(0)>0>S(1)$, so a root exists in $(0,1)$. Since $\phi \le 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, $S'(\theta) = -(1-v)\sqrt\beta\,\phi(\cdot) + \sum_j w_j\phi(g_j(\theta))(\alpha+\tau_j)/\sqrt\beta - 1 \le \sum_j w_j(\alpha+\tau_j)/\sqrt{2\pi\beta} - 1$, which is negative under the stated condition. The no-message equations are the case $v = 0$; for them uniqueness among all strategies follows from iterated deletion of dominated strategies [@angeletos2007, p. 719].

**Proposition 1.** (i) From the no-message equations, $\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \alpha(\theta_0-\mu)/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{(\alpha+\beta)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$. Hence $g(\theta_0) - \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = [\tau(\theta_0-z) - \Phi^{-1}(c)(\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\beta})]/\sqrt\beta = \tau(z_0-z)/\sqrt\beta$, and $S(\theta_0) = v[\Phi(g(\theta_0)) - \theta_0]$ has the sign of $z_0 - z$. Because $S$ is strictly decreasing, $\theta^*-\theta_0$ has the same sign. If $\theta^*>\theta_0$, then $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*)) < \theta_0 < \theta^*$, and the identity $\theta^* = (1-v)\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*)) + v\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta^*))$ forces $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta^*)) > \theta^*$, so $k>k_0$; the case $\theta^*<\theta_0$ is symmetric, and $\theta^*=\theta_0$ gives $k = k_0$. (ii) With reach zero, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $k = \theta_0 + g(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta$; subtract $k_0 = \theta_0 + \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta$. (iii) $\partial S/\partial v = \Phi(g(\theta^*)) - \Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*))$ has the sign of $k-k_0$, so $d\theta^*/dv = (\partial S/\partial v)/|\partial S/\partial\theta|$ does too, and $dk/dv = (1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)\,d\theta^*/dv$ because $k = \theta^* + g(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ and $g' = (\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta$. The cutoff therefore moves away from $k_0$ as reach rises, in the direction of $E$, and $C$ is zero at reach zero. (iv) Differentiating $S(\theta^*;z) = 0$ gives $\partial\theta^*/\partial z = -v\phi_R\tau/(\sqrt\beta\,|\partial S/\partial\theta|)$ with $|\partial S/\partial\theta| = 1 + (1-v)\sqrt\beta\phi_N - v\phi_R(\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta$, and $\partial k/\partial z = (1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)\,\partial\theta^*/\partial z - \tau/\beta$, which rearranges to the stated form. At $z = z_0$, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $g(\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0)$.

**Proposition 2.** (i) With $\tau_j = 0$ every $g_j$ equals $g_0(\theta) = \alpha(\theta-\mu)/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{(\alpha+\beta)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, and $S(\theta_0) = 0$ because $g_0(\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0)$; uniqueness gives $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $k_j = k_0$. (ii) At $\tau = 0$, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and both densities equal $\phi_0$. With $D_0 = \theta_0 - z - \Phi^{-1}(c)/(2\sqrt{\alpha+\beta})$, $\partial k(\tau,0)/\partial\tau = D_0/\beta$ and $\partial k(\tau,v)/\partial\tau = (D_0/\beta)[1 + v\phi_0(\alpha+\beta)/(\sqrt\beta + (1-v)\beta\phi_0 - v\phi_0\alpha)]$; the ratio of these derivatives is $M_0(0,v)$, and both channels are zero at $\tau = 0$. (iii) The single receiver's best response to the threshold $\theta^*$ set by the others is $k_l = \theta^* + g_l(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$; subtracting $k_h = \theta^* + g_h(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ gives the closed form, and $\partial g_l/\partial z = -\tau_l/\sqrt\beta$ gives the response. Since $\partial\theta^*/\partial z < 0$ whenever $v\tau_h > 0$, the response is negative at $\tau_l = 0$.

**Theorem 1.** (a) is Proposition 2(iii). For $c = 1/2$, $z_0 = \theta_0$ and $k_l - k_0 = (\theta^*-\theta_0)(1+\alpha/\beta) + \tau_l(\theta^*-z)/\beta$; by Proposition 1(i), if $z<\theta_0$ then $\theta^*>\theta_0>z$ and both terms are positive, and symmetrically. (b) $\partial g/\partial\tau = D/\sqrt\beta$ with $D = \theta^* - z - \Phi^{-1}(c)/(2\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta})$, so $\partial\theta^*/\partial\tau = v\phi_R D/(\sqrt\beta|\partial S/\partial\theta|)$ and $\partial k/\partial\tau = D\,[(1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)v\phi_R/(\sqrt\beta|\partial S/\partial\theta|) + 1/\beta]$ has the sign of $D$. For $c = 1/2$, $D = \theta^* - z$, which has the sign of $\theta_0 - z$ for every $\tau$. For $c \ne 1/2$, at any zero of $D$ the derivative of $\theta^*$ in $\tau$ vanishes, so $D'(\tau) = \Phi^{-1}(c)/(4(\alpha+\tau+\beta)^{3/2})$, whose sign is that of $c-1/2$; a continuous function whose derivative at each of its zeros has the same strict sign has at most one zero and crosses it in that direction. (c) For $c = 1/2$ every cutoff equals $k_0$ at $z = \theta_0$, and by Proposition 1(iv) its derivative in $z$ there is $-(\tau/\beta)M_0(\tau,v)$; the difference of two such derivatives gives the formula, and a first-order expansion around $\theta_0$ gives the sign of the disclosure's effect for nearby $z$. (d) is Proposition 2(i).

**Proposition 3.** Index the no-message period equilibrium by $u = \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$. The no-message equation gives $\mu = \Phi(u) - (\sqrt\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, strictly decreasing in $u$ when $\alpha < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$, and participation at $\theta = \mu$ is $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k^*-\mu))$ with $\sqrt\beta(k^*-\mu) = u + (\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, which is $\psi(u)$. The map $u_t \mapsto u_{t+1} = \mu^{-1}(\bar\theta - \gamma\psi(u_t))$ is strictly increasing and its fixed points are the roots of $L$. Since $\mu$ is decreasing, the map raises $u$ where $L>0$ and lowers it where $L<0$, and because it is increasing it never jumps over a fixed point. As $L \to +\infty$ when $u \to -\infty$ and $L \to -\infty$ when $u \to +\infty$, every path converges to a fixed point: with one fixed point it is globally attracting, and with three the outer two attract and the middle one separates their basins. The slope of the map at a fixed point is $\gamma\psi'/|\mu'|$, which is below one if and only if $L'<0$. For $c = 1/2$, $s = 0$ and $L'(u) = \phi(u) + \gamma(1+\beta/\alpha)\phi((1+\beta/\alpha)u) - \sqrt\beta/\alpha$ is even and decreasing in $|u|$, so $\{L'>0\}$ is an interval around zero, nonempty if and only if $L'(0)>0$, that is $\gamma>\gamma_c$; $L$ then has a local minimum and a local maximum at the zeros of $L'$, and three roots if and only if $\bar\theta$ lies strictly between the values of $\mu+\gamma\psi$ there. (ii) With a message of reach one, the period equilibrium satisfies $\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*) = [\alpha(\theta^*-\mu)+\tau(\theta^*-z)]/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, so $\theta^*$ decreases in $\mu$ and in $z$, and participation at $\theta = \mu$, $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(\theta^*-\mu) + \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*))$, decreases in both. Hence $\mu_{t+1}$ increases in $\mu_t$ and in $z$, and by induction $\mu_{T+1}$ increases strictly in $z$; the basin boundary then gives a unique critical content.

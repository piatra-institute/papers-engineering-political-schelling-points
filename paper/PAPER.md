## Abstract

When many accounts amplify a call to collective action, the message matters as evidence that the action can succeed and as a shared reference for predicting one another. A regime-change global game separates the two, with the message's perceived precision set by beliefs about its sources and a commonly believed reach distinct from actual receipt. The equilibrium cutoff decomposes exactly into a closed-form evidential channel and a coordination channel whose marginal size is the evidential response times $1/(1-\kappa v)$; at baseline coordination is 80% of the response when receipt is common knowledge. Each receiver's response depends on commonly believed receipt alone. A message every receiver believes uninformative has no effect however visible, so publicity multiplies evidence and never selects among outcomes as a pure focal point would. Disclosing that 3 accounts share one source removes 11% of their effect on receivers' participation at full reach for an isolated receiver, 71% when the disclosure is common knowledge, and intermediate amounts for labels; a public disclosure that also makes receipt common knowledge amplifies the message, to first order, exactly when a publicity-credibility ratio exceeds one. With erosion of the status quo, temporary messages persist in the noise-free limit above a closed-form feedback threshold, but under belief-consistent shocks only where shocks are small relative to erosion. A laboratory design, including a cell that varies only public acknowledgment of receipt, needs 7 to 81 groups of 20 per cell. The analysis covers campaigns perceived as at most 8 independent reports at baseline.

## A call seen by many and sent by one

A call to stop work at noon on Friday circulates in a city's feeds, posted by five accounts that look unrelated. A reader can take two different things from it. The first is evidence: if five people independently judged that the stoppage can succeed, their judgments say something about the conditions on which success depends. The second is a reference point: a reader who believes that many others have seen the same call can expect them to act on it, which makes noon on Friday the obvious moment to act. Schelling called references of the second kind focal points [@schelling1960]. A day later the platform reports that the five accounts were run from one source. The report removes most of the evidence, because five copies of one judgment carry the information of one. Its effect on the reference point depends on who learns of it. A reader who sees a label on the post and assumes that others did not can still expect them to act on the call. If the report is itself public news, every reader learns that the source was single and also that the call reached everyone.

This paper asks, for the purposes of measurement and of defence against such campaigns, how much of a message's effect on participation runs through each of these two channels, how disclosing a common source changes each, and when a temporary message leaves a change that outlasts it.

Schelling's focal points coordinate because they are shared, whatever they say about the world. The model used here, a global game of regime change, has a unique equilibrium, and in it that pure form of focality cannot occur: a message that everyone knows to be uninformative changes nothing, however public it is. What a shared message does instead is shift the unique equilibrium, and publicity multiplies the shift because each receiver expects the others to respond to the same content. This is the publicity effect of Morris and Shin [@morris2002; @morris2003], and it is the coordination channel measured here. Focal-point selection in Schelling's sense appears in two places that the paper treats separately: outside the uniqueness bound, where a message credible enough leaves several equilibria and the model cannot say which is played, and in the repeated game, where a temporary message can select which of two self-sustaining states a population ends up in.

## What is already known, and what is not

### Public information in global games

Carlsson and van Damme showed that small private noise about payoffs selects a unique equilibrium in coordination games [@carlsson1993], and Morris and Shin showed that a continuum of speculators with private signals has a unique equilibrium in a model of self-fulfilling currency attacks [@morris1998]. In the static regime-change game with a normal prior of precision $\alpha$ and normal private signals of precision $\beta$, the equilibrium is unique if and only if $\beta \ge \alpha^2/(2\pi)$, and the success threshold converges to $1-c$ as private noise vanishes [@angeletos2007, p. 719]. Hellwig showed that multiple equilibria become possible when the overall level of noise is low and public information is relatively informative [@hellwig2002]. In a beauty-contest economy Morris and Shin showed that agents give the public signal more weight than its relative precision, a reflection of the coordination motive, and that when that motive is strong more precise public information lowers welfare if private information is precise enough [@morris2002]. Their survey calls the disproportionate impact of public information a publicity effect and notes that its multiplier is largest just before the parameters enter the region of multiple equilibria [@morris2003]. Angeletos, Hellwig and Pavan showed that the information generated by a policy maker's intervention restores multiple equilibria [@angeletos2006], and that in a dynamic regime-change game with learning, multiplicity can arise under the same conditions that guarantee uniqueness in the static game, with phases of tranquillity and of attack that occur without changes in fundamentals [@angeletos2007].

### Precision and publicity as separate properties of a message

Morris and Shin argued that communicating to a group often trades the precision of the information conveyed against how commonly it is understood [@morris2007]. Myatt and Wallace gave each signal in a coordination game an accuracy, how precisely it identifies the state, and a clarity, how easy it is to understand, and showed that when actions are more complementary the information acquired in equilibrium is more public [@myatt2012]. Cornand and Heinemann studied a public signal given to each agent with some probability and the optimal degree of that publicity in a beauty-contest economy [@cornand2008]. The separation between what a message says about the state and how widely it is shared, which this paper uses, is therefore established. What these works do not study is a regime-change game in which the precision is a belief about the independence of sources, the publicity is a belief about who else received the message, and a disclosure can change both at once.

### Regime change, protest and manipulation

Edmond studied a regime that can engage in propaganda that, taken at face value, makes it seem stronger than it is; holding the number of signals fixed, the regime is harder to overthrow when each signal is precise, while adding signals, which raises both their total precision and the regime's cost of manipulation, makes it easier to overthrow unless information control has strong economies of scale [@edmond2013]. Little showed that making grievances more public has an ambiguous effect on protest, because citizens can learn that the regime is more or less popular than they expected, whereas information about logistics almost always encourages protest [@little2016]. In a second model, citizens who are not credulous still respond to propaganda because of the coordination motive, and when that motive dominates they mimic the credulous [@little2017]. Shadmehr and Bernhardt showed that when citizens are uncertain about the payoffs of revolution, public signals from other citizens can reduce the likelihood of revolt [@shadmehr2011]. Chen, Lu and Suen showed that a rumour creates a public topic on which citizens exchange views, that discussion can make a rumour more mobilizing than if everyone fully believed it, and that an extreme rumour can backfire [@chen2016]. Barberà and Jackson argued that costly protests reveal willingness to act more reliably than cheaper communication such as social media [@barbera2020]. Bueno de Mesquita modelled a vanguard whose violence manipulates beliefs about the level of anti-government sentiment; his mechanism is informational, and his solution concept rules out the vanguard creating focal points [@buenodemesquita2010].

### Information design

Bayesian persuasion and its many-player generalisation characterise what a sender who commits to an information structure can induce [@kamenica2011; @bergemann2019]. For binary-action supermodular games, Morris, Oyama and Takahashi show that implementing an outcome as the smallest equilibrium requires a sequential obedience condition, and that some outcomes require private signals [@morris2024]. Inostroza and Pavan show that, among public disclosures, the policy of a designer who fears the worst equilibrium removes strategic uncertainty by inducing all agents to take the same action [@inostroza2025]. The present paper does not solve a designer's problem. It takes a message as given and asks what its provenance and its visibility do to its effect, which is the question an observer, a platform or a regulator faces when measuring a campaign and deciding how to disclose its source.

### Common knowledge, thresholds and preference falsification

Chwe showed that a communication network helps coordination by informing later stages about earlier ones and by creating common knowledge within each stage [@chwe2000], and argued that public rituals and media events serve to produce common knowledge [@chwe2001]. Kuran argued that by suppressing their opposition to the status quo, East Europeans misled everyone, themselves included, about the chances of a successful uprising, which is why the 1989 revolutions came as a surprise [@kuran1991; @kuran1995]. Granovetter's threshold model showed that small changes in the distribution of individual thresholds can switch a population between little and wide participation [@granovetter1978], and Brock and Durlauf showed that a mean-field model of discrete choice with social interactions has multiple equilibria when the interactions exceed a threshold [@brock2001].

### Correlation neglect and the majority illusion

DeMarzo, Vayanos and Zwiebel defined persuasion bias as a failure to account for possible repetition in the information one receives [@demarzo2003]. Enke and Zimmermann found that, on average, experimental subjects treat correlated information partially as independent and so double-count a common source [@enke2019]. Levy and Razin showed that voters who underestimate the correlation between their information sources can produce better electoral outcomes, because the resulting extreme beliefs make some of them vote on information [@levy2015]. Eyster and Rabin modelled players who underestimate how other players' actions depend on their information [@eyster2005] and people who believe that each predecessor's action reflects only that person's private information [@eyster2010]. Lerman, Yan and Wu showed that network structure alone can make an attribute held by a minority appear to be held by a majority in many people's neighbourhoods, especially when low-degree nodes connect to high-degree ones [@lerman2016].

### Evidence on exposure, norms, common knowledge and focal points

Eady and colleagues linked 1496 survey respondents to the posts in their Twitter timelines around the 2016 United States election; exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency's accounts was concentrated, with 1% of users accounting for 70% of exposures, and they found no evidence of a meaningful relationship between exposure and changes in attitudes, polarization or voting behaviour [@eady2023]. Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott found that most young married men in Saudi Arabia privately support women working outside the home and underestimate other men's support, and that correcting these beliefs increased their costly willingness to help their wives search for jobs [@bursztyn2020]. Thomas, DeScioli, Haque and Pinker found that more participants attempted risky coordination when the payoffs were broadcast over a loudspeaker, and so were common knowledge, than when a messenger conveyed them to both partners or each learned them privately [@thomas2014]; De Freitas and colleagues review evidence that people are highly sensitive to the difference between common knowledge and merely private or shared knowledge [@defreitas2019]. Crawford, Gneezy and Rottenstreich found that with even slightly asymmetric payoffs, salient labels lose much of their power to coordinate [@crawford2008]. In the laboratory, Heinemann, Nagel and Ockenfels found that subjects use threshold strategies in a global game and that coordination points are about as predictable with public as with private information [@heinemann2004], and Cornand and Heinemann found that subjects put more weight on public than on private signals, but less than equilibrium predicts [@cornand2014].

### What this paper adds

The overweighting of public signals, the trade-off between precision and common understanding, the separation of a signal's accuracy from its publicness, the manipulation of signals by a regime, the leverage that credulous citizens give propaganda over sophisticated ones and the neglect of correlation are all established. The paper's addition is the regime-change, disclosure-framed version of these ideas, with closed forms. It contributes:

- an exact decomposition of a message's effect on the equilibrium cutoff into a closed-form evidential channel and a coordination channel whose marginal size is the evidential response times the multiplier $1/(1-\kappa v)$;
- a separation of actual receipt from commonly believed receipt, with the result that only the second sets a receiver's response;
- a theorem signing the effect of disclosing a common source when the disclosure is seen in isolation, when it is noticed with some probability, when it is common knowledge, and when it also makes receipt common knowledge;
- a closed-form feedback threshold for persistence and its behaviour under belief-consistent shocks;
- an identification result;
- a laboratory design with model-derived effect sizes and simulated sample sizes, including a cell that varies only public acknowledgment of receipt.

## The model

### Players, payoffs and information

A continuum of citizens of mass one chooses whether to participate in a collective action. The action succeeds if and only if the participating share $A$ is at least $\theta$, the strength of the status quo. A participant receives $1-c$ if the action succeeds and $-c$ if it fails, with $0<c<1$; abstaining pays nothing. The prior is $\theta \sim N(\mu, 1/\alpha)$, and citizen $i$ observes a private signal $x_i = \theta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1/\beta)$, independent across citizens.

### Provenance as perceived precision

A message $z$ reports on $\theta$. It is the average of $n$ reports $s_k = \theta + u_k$, each with error variance $1/\rho$ and pairwise error correlation $r$, so its precision is

$$\tau = \frac{n\rho}{1+(n-1)r}.$$

Independent sources ($r=0$) give $n\rho$; one source repeated by $n$ accounts ($r=1$) gives $\rho$. Two things can make a receiver's perceived precision differ from the true one, and the model keeps them apart. The first is ignorance of the source structure: a receiver who believes the accounts independent uses $r_b = 0$. The second is neglect of a correlation she knows: with neglect of degree $\chi$ she uses $\hat r = (1-\chi) r_b$. Before disclosure, a receiver who takes the accounts for independent sources perceives $\tau_h = n\rho$ whatever her neglect. Disclosure reveals $r_b = 1$, and a receiver who then neglects the known correlation to degree $\chi'$ perceives $\tau_l = n\rho/(1+(n-1)(1-\chi'))$, which equals $\rho$ only if $\chi' = 0$. Provenance enters the model only through perceived precision.

### Receipt and commonly believed receipt

The message actually reaches a share $v_a$ of citizens. Receivers act on a belief about who else received it: they commonly believe that a share $v$ received it, so that each receiver assigns probability $v$ to any other citizen having received it and knows that the other receivers hold the same belief. I call $v$ the commonly believed reach, or reach for short. Receivers may differ in perceived precision: group $j$ has mass $w_j$ and perceived precision $\tau_j$, with $\sum_j w_j = v$, and these masses and precisions are part of the common belief. Non-receivers do not condition on the message and play the equilibrium of the game without it; the experimental section checks this simplification against a common-prior version in which non-receivers know that a message was shown to some share but not what it said. In equilibrium $v_a = v$. Separating them shows which of the two quantities sets behaviour: actual receipt is what impressions count, and commonly believed receipt is what public acknowledgment creates.

### Equilibrium

Write $\Phi$ and $\phi$ for the standard normal distribution and density. Without a message, the success threshold $\theta_0$ and the participation cutoff $k_0$ satisfy

$$\Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt\beta}(\theta_0-\mu) - \sqrt{1+\alpha/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c), \qquad k_0 = \theta_0 + \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta,$$

and citizens participate when $x_i \le k_0$ [@angeletos2007]. With a message, a group-$j$ receiver participates when $x_i \le k_j$. Define

$$g_j(\theta) = \frac{\alpha(\theta-\mu)+\tau_j(\theta-z)}{\sqrt\beta} - \sqrt{\frac{\alpha+\tau_j+\beta}{\beta}}\,\Phi^{-1}(c),$$

$$S(\theta) = (1-v)\,\Phi\big(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta)\big) + \sum_j w_j\,\Phi\big(g_j(\theta)\big) - \theta.$$

**Lemma 1.** In every equilibrium in cutoff strategies the action succeeds if and only if $\theta \le \theta^*$, where $S(\theta^*)=0$, and each group's cutoff is $k_j = \theta^* + g_j(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$. A root exists in $[0,1]$, and it is unique when $\sum_j w_j(\alpha+\tau_j) < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$; with one group of full reach this is the classic condition $(\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta \le \sqrt{2\pi}$.

The proof (appendix) uses the fact that each group's indifference condition pins its cutoff to $\theta^*$, so the equilibrium reduces to one equation however many receiver groups there are. The receivers' cutoff is the measure of participation used throughout: the share of receivers who participate at any fundamental rises with it. Effects are reported in percentage points of receivers' participation at $\theta=\theta_0$, where participation without the message equals $\theta_0$; shares of an effect are reported on this scale and, where they differ, on the cutoff scale.

The baseline sets $\alpha = 4$, $\mu = 0.6$, $\beta = 25$ and $c = 0.5$: the public expects the action to need 60% participation, with a prior standard deviation of 0.5, and each citizen's private signal is more precise than the prior. One report has precision $\rho = 1$. Without a message, $\theta_0 = 0.453$, so the action succeeds with prior probability 0.385. The baseline mobilizing message reports $z = \theta_0 - 0.3$.

### How large a campaign the analysis covers

With full reach, uniqueness requires $\tau \le \sqrt{2\pi\beta} - \alpha = 8.53$ at baseline, so a campaign believed to consist of independent reports of precision 1 is covered only up to 8 accounts. Larger campaigns believed independent leave the region in which the results below hold. At perceived precision 9 the full-reach game has several equilibria for 0.5% of message contents within one unit of $\theta_0$; at 20, 16%; at 40, 29%; at 80, 37%, including the baseline message, at which the game then has 3 equilibria. In that region a message credible enough can act as a Schelling point in the strict sense, since which equilibrium is played is not pinned down by the model. A campaign of 80 accounts repeating one report stays inside the bound only if receivers know the accounts are dependent and neglect that dependence to a degree $\chi \le 0.894$; a disclosure that brings perceived precision below the bound restores uniqueness.

## Evidential and coordination channels

The message's effect on a receiver's cutoff has two parts. The evidential channel is the change in the cutoff of a receiver who updates her belief about $\theta$ but expects everyone else to behave as without the message; it equals the effect at reach zero. The coordination channel is the rest: the additional change that occurs because others who saw the message also respond to it, and she knows it.

**Proposition 1.** Take one receiver group with perceived precision $\tau>0$ and reach $v \in (0,1]$, with a unique equilibrium. Let

$$z_0(\tau) = \theta_0 - \Phi^{-1}(c)\,\frac{\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\beta}}{\tau}.$$

(i) For every reach, $k - k_0$ has the sign of $z_0(\tau) - z$ and is zero at $z = z_0(\tau)$: a message reporting a weaker status quo than $z_0$ raises participation, a message reporting a stronger one lowers it.

(ii) The evidential channel is $E = \tau\,[z_0(\tau)-z]/\beta$.

(iii) The coordination channel $C = k - k_0 - E$ has the sign of $E$, and $|C|$ increases strictly with reach.

(iv) The cutoff's response to content is $\partial k/\partial z = -(\tau/\beta)\,M$, where

$$M = 1 + \frac{v\,\phi_R\,(\alpha+\tau+\beta)}{\sqrt\beta + (1-v)\,\beta\,\phi_N - v\,\phi_R\,(\alpha+\tau)},$$

with $\phi_R = \phi(g(\theta^*))$ and $\phi_N = \phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*))$. At $z=z_0$ both densities equal $\phi_0 = \phi(\Phi^{-1}(\theta_0))$, and the multiplier reduces to

$$M_0(\tau, v) = \frac{1}{1-\kappa v}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\phi_0(\alpha+\tau+\beta)}{\sqrt\beta + \beta\phi_0},$$

so that at the neutral message the coordination share of the marginal response, $1 - 1/M_0$, equals $\kappa v$.

The term $\tau/\beta$ is the Bayesian weight on the message relative to the private signal, and $M$ multiplies it; $M$ is the marginal multiplier at a given content, and the coordination channel at a distance from $z_0$ is the evidential channel times the average of $M - 1$ over the contents in between. At full reach $\kappa < 1$ is the uniqueness condition at $\theta_0$, and the multiplier grows without bound as $\kappa$ approaches one. $M_0$ is the regime-change counterpart of the overweighting of public signals in Morris and Shin's beauty contest [@morris2002] and of the publicity multiplier in their survey [@morris2003], written as a function of reach.

At baseline, with $\tau = 1$, $\kappa = 0.797$, so at the neutral message the coordination share of the marginal response is $\kappa v$: 20% at reach 0.25, 40% at reach 0.5 and 80% at full reach. For the baseline message the evidential channel raises receivers' participation by 2.38 percentage points; the total effect is 2.98 points at reach 0.25, 3.98 at reach 0.5 and 11.83 at full reach (Figure 1a), and the coordination shares of the cutoff shift are 20%, 40% and 80%. The exact multiplier at the baseline message is 4.98 at full reach, against 4.94 for the closed form at the neutral message. Across 2592 parameter combinations (two prior precisions, three private precisions, three costs, two prior means, three message precisions, six message contents and four reaches) the sign result, the closed form for $E$, the sign and monotonicity of $C$ and the multiplier formula held without exception; the largest error of the closed-form evidential channel was $3.9 \times 10^{-16}$ and the largest relative error of the multiplier against finite differences was $3.4 \times 10^{-8}$. The identity $M_0 = 1/(1-\kappa v)$ held to $1.8 \times 10^{-15}$. In addition, 20 equilibria were checked by computing best responses from a numerical integral of the posterior and iterating them from "nobody participates" and "everybody participates"; in this supermodular game the two sequences bound every equilibrium, and both converged to the analytic cutoffs within $5.3 \times 10^{-10}$.

**Proposition 2.** (i) If every receiver assigns the message zero precision, every cutoff equals $k_0$ for every reach. (ii) As $\tau \to 0$, both channels vanish but $C/E \to M_0(0,v)-1 > 0$. (iii) A single receiver who assigns precision $\tau_l$ while the others assign $\tau_h$ has cutoff $k_l = k_h - [(\tau_h-\tau_l)(\theta^*-z) - \Phi^{-1}(c)(\sqrt{\alpha+\tau_h+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\tau_l+\beta})]/\beta$ and response to content $\partial k_l/\partial z = (1+(\alpha+\tau_l)/\beta)\,\partial\theta^*/\partial z - \tau_l/\beta$, which is negative even at $\tau_l = 0$ whenever $v>0$ and $\tau_h>0$.

Part (i) is the global-game reason why a known sunspot cannot coordinate when the equilibrium is unique: a message believed uninformative is ignored by everyone, so it predicts nothing. Within the uniqueness region, coordination value therefore multiplies evidential value and cannot exist without it somewhere in the belief hierarchy. Part (iii) locates where it can survive: a receiver who regards the message as worthless still follows it because the others, who have not learned what she has, follow it. This is the mechanism of Little's credulous citizens [@little2017], here produced by provenance information that is not common knowledge. At baseline, with $\tau = 0.0001$, the computed ratio $C/E$ is 3.364 against the limit 3.364. The closed form in (iii) matched the computed cutoffs to within $4.2 \times 10^{-17}$.

**Proposition 3 (receipt versus commonly believed receipt).** If a share $v_a$ receives the message while receivers commonly believe the share is $v$, each receiver's cutoff, and hence her response to content, is the reach-$v$ equilibrium cutoff of Lemma 1 and does not depend on $v_a$. The change in aggregate participation at any fundamental is $v_a$ times the receivers' change.

Actual receipt therefore scales the effect linearly, and commonly believed receipt sets it through the multiplier. At baseline, a message that everyone receives but whose receipt is commonly believed only to degree 0.25 raises participation by 2.98 points, while a message received by a share 0.25 of citizens who believe everyone received it raises aggregate participation by 2.96 points, with each receiver moving by 11.83. When everyone receives it and knows that everyone did, the effect is 11.83 points. Counting impressions measures $v_a$ and says nothing about $v$.

## Disclosure of a common source

Let disclosure lower receivers' perceived precision from $\tau_h$ to $\tau_l$. For $n$ accounts repeating one report that receivers took for independent sources, $\tau_h = n\rho$ and $\tau_l = n\rho/(1+(n-1)(1-\chi'))$, where $\chi'$ is the neglect of the disclosed correlation; the examples below take $\chi' = 0$ unless stated.

**Theorem 1 (publicity paradox).**

(a) *Isolated disclosure.* A receiver who alone learns the source changes her cutoff by the closed-form amount in Proposition 2(iii), at an unchanged success threshold. For $c = 1/2$ the change never reverses the sign of the message's effect.

(b) *Common disclosure at unchanged reach.* If all receivers learn the source and this is common knowledge among them, then for $c = 1/2$ participation falls if $z < \theta_0$, is unchanged if $z = \theta_0$ and rises if $z > \theta_0$. For any $c$, the cutoff is single-peaked in perceived precision when $c<1/2$ and single-troughed when $c>1/2$, so disclosure lowers (raises) participation whenever $\partial k/\partial\tau$ is positive (negative) at both $\tau_l$ and $\tau_h$.

(c) *Public disclosure that raises reach.* If disclosure also raises reach from $v_0$ to $v_1$, define the publicity-credibility ratio

$$\Pi = \frac{\tau_l\, M_0(\tau_l, v_1)}{\tau_h\, M_0(\tau_h, v_0)}.$$

For $c = 1/2$, $\partial[k(\tau_l,v_1) - k(\tau_h,v_0)]/\partial z = (\tau_h M_0(\tau_h,v_0) - \tau_l M_0(\tau_l,v_1))/\beta$ at $z = \theta_0$. Hence for messages near $\theta_0$ disclosure amplifies the message's effect, raising participation in a mobilizing message and lowering it in a demobilizing one, if $\Pi > 1$, attenuates it if $\Pi < 1$, and leaves it unchanged to first order if $\Pi = 1$.

(d) *Uninformative source.* If disclosure makes every receiver regard the message as uninformative and this is common knowledge, the message has no effect at any reach.

Part (c) is the regime-change form of the trade-off between precision and common understanding that Morris and Shin describe for communication to a group [@morris2007]; $\Pi$ is the ratio of the marginal responses after and before disclosure, so the criterion is a first-order statement, exact for small surprises.

Isolated disclosure removes the evidential difference and leaves the coordination channel in place. Common disclosure removes more than in proportion, because the multiplier itself rises with perceived precision. At baseline with 3 accounts ($\tau_h = 3$) and full reach, the message raises receivers' participation by 40.66 points. An isolated disclosure that leaves the disclosed receiver with precision 1 removes 11% of this effect on her (to 36.17 points; 15% on the cutoff scale), and 17% if she regards the message as uninformative (to 33.62 points). The same disclosure made common knowledge removes 71% (to 11.83 points; 75% on the cutoff scale): the evidential channel falls by 67% and the coordination channel by 77%, and the ratio $C/E$ falls from 5.65 to 3.96. At reach 0.25 an isolated disclosure removes 54% of a smaller effect, because less of it was coordination. The single-peakedness in (b) held on all 358 computed paths of 80 perceived precisions each across the parameter grid of the previous section.

A label attached to a post lies between these cases. A receiver who notices it does not know whether others did; if each receiver notices independently with probability $p$, commonly known, the noticers and the rest form two receiver groups and Lemma 1 gives the equilibrium. For the noticer, the share of the effect removed rises from 11% at $p$ near zero to 27% at $p = 0.25$, 42% at $p = 0.5$, 57% at $p = 0.75$ and 71% at $p = 1$ (Figure 1d). The isolated figure is therefore a lower bound on what a label does, attained only when a reader is sure that no one else noticed it.

Public disclosure can raise participation. With 3 accounts, a campaign seen with a commonly believed reach $v_0 = 0.25$ raises receivers' participation by 9.11 points; a public disclosure that lowers perceived precision to 1 and makes receipt common knowledge raises it to 11.83 points ($\Pi = 1.30$), while the same disclosure at unchanged reach lowers it to 2.98. With 5 accounts the campaign raises participation by 15.34 points and public disclosure lowers it to 11.83 ($\Pi = 0.76$). Whether the paradox occurs depends on how much of the disclosed correlation receivers still neglect: with 5 accounts it occurs whenever $\chi'$ exceeds 0.260, and at $\chi' = 0.5$ the same disclosure raises participation to 21.23 points ($\Pi = 1.40$). With 3 accounts it occurs at every $\chi'$. Figure 1c shows the region at baseline as a function of the deflation $\tau_h/\tau_l$. The first-order criterion predicted the sign of the exact effect at all 1438 parameter points with surprise $|z-\theta_0| = 0.01$, and at 99.7% of 1440 points with surprise 0.3 (two prior precisions, three private precisions, two values of $\tau_l$, six deflation factors, ten values of $v_0$ and both directions of surprise). The 3 points with multiple equilibria were excluded.

## Persistence after the message is gone

A message that changes participation for as long as it lasts need not leave anything behind. A persistent change requires a feedback from today's participation to tomorrow's conditions. The repeated version describes a recurring action, such as a weekly stoppage, whose success in one period weakens the status quo without abolishing it. Each period is the static game with public prior mean $\mu_t$, and participation erodes the status quo's expected strength:

$$\mu_{t+1} = \bar\theta - \gamma A_t.$$

The fundamental each period is $\theta_t = \mu_t + \xi_t$ with $\xi_t \sim N(0, 1/\alpha)$, so the citizens' prior is correct. Its deterministic skeleton sets $\xi_t = 0$. Without a message the period equilibrium can be indexed by $u = \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$, with $\mu(u) = \Phi(u) - (\sqrt\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, $s = \sqrt{1+\alpha/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, and skeleton participation $\psi(u) = \Phi((1+\beta/\alpha)u + \beta s/\alpha)$. Steady states of the skeleton are the roots of $L(u) = \mu(u) + \gamma\psi(u) - \bar\theta$, and a steady state is stable if and only if $L'(u)<0$.

**Proposition 4.** Assume static uniqueness, $\alpha < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$. (i) For $c = 1/2$, the skeleton has two stable steady states if and only if

$$\gamma > \gamma_c = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}-\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$$

and $\bar\theta$ lies strictly between the values of $\mu+\gamma\psi$ at the two zeros of $L'$; otherwise the steady state is unique and globally attracting, and every temporary message's effect decays. (ii) In the bistable case a campaign that ends after period $T$ leaves the skeleton in the high-participation state if and only if $\mu_{T+1}$ is below the unstable steady state; for a campaign of fixed content, perceived precision and reach there is a critical content $z_c$ such that it persists if and only if $z < z_c$.

The threshold falls as public information becomes more precise relative to private information and vanishes as $\beta$ grows: in the limit of precise private signals participation is all or nothing, and any erosion creates two states. The condition plays the role of Brock and Durlauf's bistability condition for mean-field social interactions [@brock2001], in a game whose static equilibrium is unique in every period.

At baseline $\gamma_c = 0.294$, equal to the numerically computed threshold to machine precision; for $c = 0.3$ and $c = 0.7$ the numerical threshold is 0.310. On a grid of 1640 pairs $(\gamma, \bar\theta)$ the number of steady states matched the characterisation everywhere. With $\gamma = 0.6$ and $\bar\theta = 0.8$, the middle of the bistable interval, the low state has expected strength 0.796 and participation 0.006, the unstable state 0.5 and 0.5, and the high state expected strength 0.204 and participation 0.994, so both states have a status quo of positive strength. In the skeleton, a campaign of 4 periods with perceived precision 3 persists if its content is below $z_c = 0.107$; content just below the threshold leaves the system in the high state and content just above returns it to the low state (Figure 2b). Longer campaigns need less extreme content, and a campaign assigned precision 1 needs content below $-0.76$ (Figure 2c). The same campaign at $\gamma = 0.8\gamma_c$ decays to the unique steady state. In a campaign of 6 periods whose undisclosed version crosses the unstable state after 4 periods, the change persists if the source is disclosed after 4 or more periods and dies out if it is disclosed after 3 or fewer.

## What data can and cannot identify

**Proposition 5.** (i) In data on exposure to a single campaign and subsequent action, with exposure possibly randomized, four explanations are observationally equivalent: private evidence (a message seen with no shared visibility), coordination (a message whose receipt is commonly believed), private valuation persuasion and a majority illusion. Each acts on exposed citizens only through their cutoff, and each can produce any cutoff in an interval around $k_0$. (ii) With many messages of known content, data from the coordination game identify, at the neutral message, only the product $\tau M_0(\tau,v)$: pairs $(\tau, v)$ with equal products have equal responses there. Away from the neutral message the curvature of $k(z)$ separates them, but weakly. (iii) A design that adds a task whose payoffs do not depend on others identifies $\tau/\beta$ from the response to content in that task, and the ratio of the responses in the coordination and individual tasks identifies $M$.

At baseline the full-reach campaign with $\tau = 1$ raises exposed participation by 11.83 points. The same exposed behaviour is produced by a privately seen message with perceived precision 4.96, by persuasion that lowers the cost of participating by 0.109, by a neglected bias of 0.060 in private signals, or by a belief that others' cutoff is 0.077 higher than it is. At the neutral message, precision 1 with full reach has the same response as precision 2.85 at reach 0.5 and 4.94 at reach zero (Figure 3c). Globally the pairs differ, but little: over contents within 2 standard deviations of the bulletin, participation at $\theta_0$ differs from the reference pair by at most 1.45 points for the zero-reach alternative and 0.42 points for the half-reach one. In laboratory data where $\theta$ and $z$ are observed and choices are free of noise, the Kullback-Leibler divergence per choice is $2.4 \times 10^{-4}$ and $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$, so 12,233 and 259,066 choices would be needed for an expected likelihood ratio of 20. A credible message seen privately and a discredited message seen by everyone, the two states that the publicity paradox compares, are close to indistinguishable from responses alone.

## A decisive experiment

### Design

Groups of 20 participants play 30 rounds of the game with the baseline parameters as its actual distributions: in each round the computer draws $\theta$, every participant's private signal and one bulletin of precision 1. Participants receive no feedback on $\theta$, on outcomes or on the bulletin's accuracy until the session ends, and one round is paid, so that beliefs about the bulletin's precision cannot be learned from experience during the session. Two factors vary between groups. Incentive: in the coordination task participation pays if the group's participation share reaches $\theta$; in the individual task it pays if $\theta$ is below an announced hurdle equal to $\theta_0$, so that others' choices are irrelevant and the model's cutoff equals the evidential cutoff. Visibility: in the public condition every member sees the bulletin on a shared screen with each member's acknowledgment visible; in the partial condition a random share $q = 0.25$ of members see it and are told that this share saw it, while the others are told that a bulletin was shown to that share. Both conditions are truthful. The outcome is each receiver's choice; the estimand is the visibility-by-incentive interaction in the slope of participation on bulletin content, estimated group by group.

The model's predictions are exact equilibria: the public coordination cell from Lemma 1, the partial coordination cell from the common-prior version with informed non-receivers, the individual task in closed form. Participants are allowed to fall short of equilibrium: their cutoff is the evidential cutoff plus a fraction $\omega$ of the coordination component, with logistic decision noise (scale 0.1), participant heterogeneity (standard deviation 0.1) and group heterogeneity (0.05) in cutoffs. Laboratory studies of public and private signals find subjects reacting less to public information than equilibrium predicts [@cornand2014], which is why $\omega<1$ is the relevant range.

### Predictions and power

Under equilibrium play the response of participation to one standard deviation of bulletin content (1.12) is 12.17 points in the public coordination cell against 2.89 to 3.58 points in the other three cells of the main design (Figure 3a), and the interaction is 8.64 points. It is 4.75 points at $\omega = 0.5$ and 2.56 points at $\omega = 0.25$. Sample sizes were found by simulation: starting from a noncentral-t approximation that uses the simulated between-group variances, the number of groups was raised until the rejection rate of a two-sided 5% test in 2000 simulated experiments reached 80%.

### Receipt held fixed, acknowledgment varied

The visibility factor changes how many members receive the bulletin together with whether receipt is acknowledged. A second contrast holds receipt fixed. Every member receives the bulletin; in one condition it appears on a shared screen with visible acknowledgments, in the other on private screens, each accompanied by a truthful private statement that every member received the same bulletin. This follows Thomas and colleagues' contrast between common knowledge by loudspeaker and shared knowledge by messenger [@thomas2014]. In the model the private condition corresponds to a commonly believed reach $p$ below one, which the session can elicit through second-order beliefs, and Proposition 3 predicts that the response to content depends on $p$ alone.

Under equilibrium play the private condition's response to content is 3.58 points per standard deviation at $p = 0.25$, 4.80 at $p = 0.5$ and 6.88 at $p = 0.75$, against 12.17 in the public condition. Crossed with the incentive, so that any effect of the shared screen on attention appears in the individual task as well, the acknowledgment-by-incentive interaction needs 6, 8 and 14 groups per cell for 80% power at these values of $p$, and 17, 22 and 43 groups if participants act on a share $\omega = 0.5$ of the coordination component. If participants report second-order beliefs well below certainty in the private condition and their response to content nonetheless matches the public condition, the model's account of the coordination channel fails.

### Provenance arm

A third factor tests the publicity paradox directly. The bulletin is presented as the average of $n = 5$ accounts that in fact repeat one report, so its true precision is 1. In the undisclosed condition participants see only that the accounts posted, and the prediction assumes they take them for independent sources ($\tau_h = 5$); in the disclosed condition they are told that all of them copied one report, and their remaining neglect $\chi'$ sets $\tau_l$. Both perceived precisions are measured in the individual task, through the response to content with and without disclosure. Comparing the undisclosed bulletin under partial visibility with the disclosed bulletin under public visibility reproduces the two states of Theorem 1(c). With $\chi' = 0$ the ratio $\Pi$ is 0.76, both in closed form and when computed from the informed-non-receiver model used for the cells, and the undisclosed partial bulletin draws a response 2.56 points per standard deviation larger, detectable with 43 groups per condition; with $\chi' = 0.5$, $\Pi = 1.40$ and the disclosed public bulletin draws a response 3.41 points larger, detectable with 30 groups. Near $\chi' = 0.260$ the difference vanishes. The model thus predicts a sign reversal at a level of neglect that the same experiment measures. Because participants receive no feedback during the session, they cannot learn the undisclosed bulletin's true precision as rounds pass; with feedback the prediction for later rounds would move toward the disclosed case.

### Approximations

Two simplifications were checked. In the common-prior version with informed non-receivers, the non-receivers' participation at $\theta_0$ moves by at most 1.06 points and receivers' predicted participation differs from the simpler model by at most 0.44 points, 1.8% of the largest effect, over two precisions, four reaches and thirteen message contents; a Monte Carlo check with 400,000 draws put the non-receivers' success probability at their cutoff at 0.5001 against the required 0.5. In groups of 20 the symmetric equilibrium of the public task, computed with the binomial distribution of others' choices, has a response to content equal to 96% of the continuum value and a participation level 10.3 points higher at $\theta_0$, because each participant's own choice counts toward the threshold; with groups of 50 the response is 98.5% of the continuum value and the level 4.2 points higher. In the partial task with 5 receivers and 15 informed non-receivers per group of 20, the finite equilibrium's response is also 96% of the continuum value, so the interaction in cutoff responses is 96% of its continuum value and the sample sizes above, computed with continuum cutoffs, are slightly optimistic.

## Implications for measurement, detection and disclosure

Measure provenance, receipt and common belief separately. In the model the number of accounts acts only through perceived precision, actual receipt scales the aggregate effect linearly, and the coordination channel depends on commonly believed receipt. Counts of posts or accounts measure none of these, and counts of impressions measure only actual receipt. An audit of a suspected campaign needs three estimates: the dependence among its sources, which methods that build coordination networks from behavioural traces shared among accounts are designed to supply [@pacheco2021]; the share who saw it; and the extent to which recipients could know that others saw the same content, for instance because it appeared in a shared trending list or was rebroadcast. The third quantity determines the multiplier, and it is rarely measured.

Treat labels and announcements as different interventions. A label is noticed by some viewers and not others, and a viewer rarely knows who else noticed it. In the model its effect lies between an isolated disclosure, which removes 11% of the effect at full reach for the viewer who notices it, and a disclosure that is common knowledge among receivers, which removes 71%; it reaches the upper figure only as the probability that others noticed approaches one. A disclosure that is public news can also raise reach, and then its effect is signed by the publicity-credibility ratio. A disclosure raises reach only to the extent that it conveys the message's content to people who had not seen it or makes its receipt commonly known; one that names the source to those who already received the message, and makes that common knowledge among them, removes coordination value without adding reach.

Time detection relative to the crossing, where the environment makes crossing matter. In the skeleton a disclosure that arrives after participation has passed the unstable state does not undo the change. Under belief-consistent shocks this holds where shocks are small relative to erosion, as in the precise environment; at baseline the states mix within a few periods and timing matters little.

Read exposure studies as specific to a commonly believed reach. An exposure effect estimated in a population where the message was seen privately measures the evidential channel plus a small coordination channel. It does not bound the effect of the same messages when receipt is common knowledge, and observational data recover the multiplier only weakly. Designs that vary acknowledgment of receipt, or natural variation in whether content became common knowledge, are needed.

Correct impressions of independent support where receipt is common knowledge. Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott's evidence that correcting misperceived norms changes behaviour [@bursztyn2020] has a counterpart in the model: a correction that each person receives privately leaves the others' expected behaviour unchanged, so it removes less than a correction whose receipt is common knowledge. Institutions that publish verified counts of independent participation in a channel everyone knows everyone watches supply the common-knowledge form of correction.

## Scope

The model has one binary action, normal information, a continuum of citizens and cutoff strategies. Uniqueness is established among cutoff strategies under the condition of Lemma 1 and, for 20 configurations, among all strategies by iterated best responses from the extreme profiles. It binds the size of campaign the analysis covers: at baseline, 8 reports believed independent. Commonly believed reach is a single number shared by receivers; richer higher-order structures, such as receivers who know that everyone received the message but doubt that others know this, are represented only through it. Non-receivers ignore the message in the main model; the common-prior version with informed non-receivers changes receivers' predicted participation by at most 1.8% of the largest effect. The message is exogenous: the sender's choice of content, timing and amplification is not modelled, competing messages are not modelled, and the paper makes no welfare claims. The exact global statements about disclosure are for $c = 1/2$; for other costs the paper gives the single-peakedness lemma and computes the rest. The repeated model treats a recurring action whose success erodes but does not end the status quo; persistence is exact in the skeleton and, under belief-consistent shocks, substantial only where shocks are small relative to erosion. The parameter values are illustrative and are not calibrated to any population; the shares and magnitudes reported describe the baseline only. The experiment is a design: its behavioural parameters are assumptions, and its predictions are model derivations.

## Appendix: proofs

**Lemma 1.** Fix cutoffs $k_j$ for receivers and $k_0$ for non-receivers. Participation $A(\theta) = (1-v)\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta)) + \sum_j w_j\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_j-\theta))$ is strictly decreasing in $\theta$, so $A(\theta)-\theta$ has a unique zero $\hat\theta$ and the action succeeds if and only if $\theta \le \hat\theta$. A group-$j$ receiver with signal $x$ has posterior $\theta \mid x,z \sim N((\alpha\mu+\tau_j z+\beta x)/P_j,\,1/P_j)$ with $P_j = \alpha+\tau_j+\beta$, so her probability of success, $\Phi(\sqrt{P_j}(\hat\theta - (\alpha\mu+\tau_j z+\beta x)/P_j))$, is strictly decreasing in $x$, and her best response is the cutoff at which it equals $c$: $\beta k_j = P_j\hat\theta - \alpha\mu - \tau_j z - \sqrt{P_j}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, that is, $\sqrt\beta(k_j-\hat\theta) = g_j(\hat\theta)$. Substituting into $A(\hat\theta) = \hat\theta$ gives $S(\hat\theta) = 0$; conversely, for any root $\theta^*$ of $S$ the cutoffs $\theta^* + g_j(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ are mutual best responses. $S$ is continuous with $S(0)>0>S(1)$, so a root exists in $(0,1)$. Since $\phi \le 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, $S'(\theta) = -(1-v)\sqrt\beta\,\phi(\cdot) + \sum_j w_j\phi(g_j(\theta))(\alpha+\tau_j)/\sqrt\beta - 1 \le \sum_j w_j(\alpha+\tau_j)/\sqrt{2\pi\beta} - 1$, which is negative under the stated condition. The no-message equations are the case $v = 0$; for them uniqueness among all strategies follows from iterated deletion of dominated strategies [@angeletos2007, p. 719].

**Proposition 1.** (i) From the no-message equations, $\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \alpha(\theta_0-\mu)/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{(\alpha+\beta)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$. Hence $g(\theta_0) - \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = [\tau(\theta_0-z) - \Phi^{-1}(c)(\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha+\beta})]/\sqrt\beta = \tau(z_0-z)/\sqrt\beta$, and $S(\theta_0) = v[\Phi(g(\theta_0)) - \theta_0]$ has the sign of $z_0 - z$. Because $S$ is strictly decreasing, $\theta^*-\theta_0$ has the same sign. If $\theta^*>\theta_0$, then $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*)) < \theta_0 < \theta^*$, and the identity $\theta^* = (1-v)\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*)) + v\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta^*))$ forces $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta^*)) > \theta^*$, so $k>k_0$; the case $\theta^*<\theta_0$ is symmetric, and $\theta^*=\theta_0$ gives $k = k_0$. (ii) With reach zero, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $k = \theta_0 + g(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta$; subtract $k_0 = \theta_0 + \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0)/\sqrt\beta$. (iii) $\partial S/\partial v = \Phi(g(\theta^*)) - \Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta^*))$ has the sign of $k-k_0$, so $d\theta^*/dv = (\partial S/\partial v)/|\partial S/\partial\theta|$ does too, and $dk/dv = (1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)\,d\theta^*/dv$ because $k = \theta^* + g(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ and $g' = (\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta$. The cutoff therefore moves away from $k_0$ as reach rises, in the direction of $E$, and $C$ is zero at reach zero. (iv) Differentiating $S(\theta^*;z) = 0$ gives $\partial\theta^*/\partial z = -v\phi_R\tau/(\sqrt\beta\,|\partial S/\partial\theta|)$ with $|\partial S/\partial\theta| = 1 + (1-v)\sqrt\beta\phi_N - v\phi_R(\alpha+\tau)/\sqrt\beta$, and $\partial k/\partial z = (1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)\,\partial\theta^*/\partial z - \tau/\beta$, which rearranges to the stated form. At $z = z_0$, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $g(\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0)$, so both densities equal $\phi_0$. The denominator of $M_0 - 1$ is then $\sqrt\beta + \beta\phi_0 - v\phi_0(\alpha+\tau+\beta)$; adding the numerator $v\phi_0(\alpha+\tau+\beta)$ gives $\sqrt\beta + \beta\phi_0$, so $M_0 = (\sqrt\beta+\beta\phi_0)/(\sqrt\beta+\beta\phi_0 - v\phi_0(\alpha+\tau+\beta)) = 1/(1-\kappa v)$.

**Proposition 2.** (i) With $\tau_j = 0$ every $g_j$ equals $g_0(\theta) = \alpha(\theta-\mu)/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{(\alpha+\beta)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, and $S(\theta_0) = 0$ because $g_0(\theta_0) = \Phi^{-1}(\theta_0) = \sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta_0)$; uniqueness gives $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and $k_j = k_0$. (ii) At $\tau = 0$, $\theta^* = \theta_0$ and both densities equal $\phi_0$. With $D_0 = \theta_0 - z - \Phi^{-1}(c)/(2\sqrt{\alpha+\beta})$, $\partial k(\tau,0)/\partial\tau = D_0/\beta$ and $\partial k(\tau,v)/\partial\tau = (D_0/\beta)[1 + v\phi_0(\alpha+\beta)/(\sqrt\beta + (1-v)\beta\phi_0 - v\phi_0\alpha)]$; the ratio of these derivatives is $M_0(0,v)$, and both channels are zero at $\tau = 0$. (iii) The single receiver's best response to the threshold $\theta^*$ set by the others is $k_l = \theta^* + g_l(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$; subtracting $k_h = \theta^* + g_h(\theta^*)/\sqrt\beta$ gives the closed form, and $\partial g_l/\partial z = -\tau_l/\sqrt\beta$ gives the response. Since $\partial\theta^*/\partial z < 0$ whenever $v\tau_h > 0$, the response is negative at $\tau_l = 0$.

**Theorem 1.** (a) is Proposition 2(iii). For $c = 1/2$, $z_0 = \theta_0$ and $k_l - k_0 = (\theta^*-\theta_0)(1+\alpha/\beta) + \tau_l(\theta^*-z)/\beta$; by Proposition 1(i), if $z<\theta_0$ then $\theta^*>\theta_0>z$ and both terms are positive, and symmetrically. (b) $\partial g/\partial\tau = D/\sqrt\beta$ with $D = \theta^* - z - \Phi^{-1}(c)/(2\sqrt{\alpha+\tau+\beta})$, so $\partial\theta^*/\partial\tau = v\phi_R D/(\sqrt\beta|\partial S/\partial\theta|)$ and $\partial k/\partial\tau = D\,[(1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta)v\phi_R/(\sqrt\beta|\partial S/\partial\theta|) + 1/\beta]$ has the sign of $D$. For $c = 1/2$, $D = \theta^* - z$, which has the sign of $\theta_0 - z$ for every $\tau$. For $c \ne 1/2$, at any zero of $D$ the derivative of $\theta^*$ in $\tau$ vanishes, so $D'(\tau) = \Phi^{-1}(c)/(4(\alpha+\tau+\beta)^{3/2})$, whose sign is that of $c-1/2$; a continuous function whose derivative at each of its zeros has the same strict sign has at most one zero and crosses it in that direction. (c) For $c = 1/2$ every cutoff equals $k_0$ at $z = \theta_0$, and by Proposition 1(iv) its derivative in $z$ there is $-(\tau/\beta)M_0(\tau,v)$; the difference of two such derivatives gives the formula, and a first-order expansion around $\theta_0$ gives the sign of the disclosure's effect for nearby $z$. (d) is Proposition 2(i).

**Proposition 3.** Receivers choose cutoffs given their beliefs about others' strategies; under the common belief that a share $v$ received the message and uses the receivers' cutoff, while the rest use $k_0$, the argument of Lemma 1 applies with $v$ in place of the actual share, so the cutoff does not depend on $v_a$. Aggregate participation at $\theta$ is $(1-v_a)\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta)) + v_a\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta))$, whose change from the no-message level is $v_a[\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k-\theta)) - \Phi(\sqrt\beta(k_0-\theta))]$.

**Proposition 4.** Index the no-message period equilibrium by $u = \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*)$. The no-message equation gives $\mu = \Phi(u) - (\sqrt\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, strictly decreasing in $u$ when $\alpha < \sqrt{2\pi\beta}$, and participation at $\theta = \mu$ is $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(k^*-\mu))$ with $\sqrt\beta(k^*-\mu) = u + (\beta/\alpha)(u+s)$, which is $\psi(u)$. The map $u_t \mapsto u_{t+1} = \mu^{-1}(\bar\theta - \gamma\psi(u_t))$ is strictly increasing and its fixed points are the roots of $L$. Since $\mu$ is decreasing, the map raises $u$ where $L>0$ and lowers it where $L<0$, and because it is increasing it never jumps over a fixed point. As $L \to +\infty$ when $u \to -\infty$ and $L \to -\infty$ when $u \to +\infty$, every path converges to a fixed point: with one fixed point it is globally attracting, and with three the outer two attract and the middle one separates their basins. The slope of the map at a fixed point is $\gamma\psi'/|\mu'|$, which is below one if and only if $L'<0$. For $c = 1/2$, $s = 0$ and $L'(u) = \phi(u) + \gamma(1+\beta/\alpha)\phi((1+\beta/\alpha)u) - \sqrt\beta/\alpha$ is even and decreasing in $|u|$, so $\{L'>0\}$ is an interval around zero, nonempty if and only if $L'(0)>0$, that is $\gamma>\gamma_c$; $L$ then has a local minimum and a local maximum at the zeros of $L'$, and three roots if and only if $\bar\theta$ lies strictly between the values of $\mu+\gamma\psi$ there. (ii) With a message of reach one, the period equilibrium satisfies $\Phi^{-1}(\theta^*) = [\alpha(\theta^*-\mu)+\tau(\theta^*-z)]/\sqrt\beta - \sqrt{1+(\alpha+\tau)/\beta}\,\Phi^{-1}(c)$, so $\theta^*$ decreases in $\mu$ and in $z$, and participation at $\theta = \mu$, $\Phi(\sqrt\beta(\theta^*-\mu) + \Phi^{-1}(\theta^*))$, decreases in both. Hence $\mu_{t+1}$ increases in $\mu_t$ and in $z$, and by induction $\mu_{T+1}$ increases strictly in $z$; the basin boundary then gives a unique critical content.

**Proposition 5.** (i) Exposed citizens' choices depend on the mechanism only through their cutoff, since a citizen with signal $x$ participates if and only if $x$ is below it; each mechanism's cutoff is continuous and strictly monotone in its parameter (perceived precision at reach zero by Proposition 1(ii), reach by Proposition 1(iii), the cost through the indifference condition, the bias additively), so each attains every cutoff in an interval around $k_0$. (ii) By Proposition 1(iv) the response at the neutral message is $-(\tau/\beta)M_0(\tau,v)$, and $\tau M_0(\tau,v)$ is strictly increasing in each argument, so its level sets are curves; away from $z_0$ the multiplier depends on content through $\phi_R$ and $\phi_N$, and the size of the resulting separation is computed numerically. (iii) In the individual task the cutoff is $k_E$, whose response to content is $-\tau/\beta$ by Proposition 1(ii).
